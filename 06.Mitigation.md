## Exercise 6.1: Cost the fix, and find who pays

```python
# section6_cost.py
to = ThresholdOptimizer(estimator=clf, constraints="demographic_parity",
                        objective="accuracy_score",
                        predict_method="predict_proba", prefit=True)
to.fit(X_te, y_true, sensitive_features=sex)
yh = to.predict(X_te, sensitive_features=sex, random_state=0)

before = {g: (y_hat[sex == g] == 0).mean() for g in ["male", "female"]}
after  = {g: (yh[sex == g]    == 0).mean() for g in ["male", "female"]}
print(f"accuracy  before {accuracy_score(y_true, y_hat):.3f}  "
      f"after {accuracy_score(y_true, yh):.3f}")
for g in ["male", "female"]:
    print(f"approval {g:7s}  before {before[g]:.3f}  after {after[g]:.3f}  "
          f"change {after[g]-before[g]:+.3f}")
```

Read the output carefully, because it defies the lazy assumption that fairness costs accuracy. **Accuracy barely moves** (about 0.812 to 0.818). The real cost is elsewhere: to equalise approval, both groups' approval rates fall from about 0.93 to about 0.88. The bill was paid in **declined applications**, spread across everyone, and, as Section 5 showed, in a **widened equalised-odds gap**. "Fairness was free on accuracy" and "fairness cost real people credit and worsened another fairness metric" are both true. Your assessment must say the second, not just the first.
