# Section 2: Summary statistics, and what they hide

## What You'll Learn

Three questions describe any numeric column: where is its centre, how spread out is it, and what shape is it? You'll answer all three for this data, and find a column where the mean is actively misleading.

## Exercise 2.1: Centre (mean or median?)

**Step 1:** Compare the two measures of centre on four columns:

```python
# section2_centre.py
for c in ["LIMIT_BAL", "AGE", "BILL_AMT1", "PAY_AMT1"]:
    s = df[c]
    print(f"{c:10s} mean={s.mean():>10,.0f}  median={s.median():>9,.0f}  "
          f"ratio={s.mean() / s.median():.2f}")
```

```
LIMIT_BAL  mean=   167,484  median=  140,000  ratio=1.20
AGE        mean=        35  median=       34  ratio=1.04
BILL_AMT1  mean=    51,223  median=   22,382  ratio=2.29
PAY_AMT1   mean=     5,664  median=    2,100  ratio=2.70
```

**Question:** For `AGE`, mean and median agree to within a year, so either will do. For `PAY_AMT1` the mean is **2.7 times** the median. If you reported "the average client paid 5,664 NT dollars last month", what impression would you give, and how many clients actually paid anything like that? Which number is the honest one to quote, and why?

## Exercise 2.2: Spread (and a warning about standard deviation)

**Step 1:** Put standard deviation next to the IQR:

```python
# section2_spread.py
for c in ["LIMIT_BAL", "BILL_AMT1", "PAY_AMT1"]:
    s = df[c]
    q1, q3 = s.quantile([.25, .75])
    print(f"{c:10s} sd={s.std():>10,.0f}   IQR={q3 - q1:>10,.0f}")
```

```
LIMIT_BAL  sd=   129,748   IQR=   190,000
BILL_AMT1  sd=    73,636   IQR=    63,532
PAY_AMT1   sd=    16,563   IQR=     4,006
```

**Question:** For `PAY_AMT1` the standard deviation is **four times** the IQR. The IQR describes the middle half of clients; the standard deviation is being dragged upward by the tail. Which one tells a lender more about a typical client? (Both are correct arithmetic. Only one answers the question a human is actually asking.)

## Exercise 2.3: Shape (skew and kurtosis)

**Step 1:** Measure the lopsidedness and the tail-heaviness:

```python
# section2_shape.py
for c in ["LIMIT_BAL", "AGE", "BILL_AMT1", "PAY_AMT1"]:
    print(f"{c:10s} skew={stats.skew(df[c]):>6.2f}   "
          f"excess kurtosis={stats.kurtosis(df[c]):>8.2f}")
```

```
LIMIT_BAL  skew=  0.99   excess kurtosis=    0.54
AGE        skew=  0.73   excess kurtosis=    0.04
BILL_AMT1  skew=  2.66   excess kurtosis=    9.80
PAY_AMT1   skew= 14.67   excess kurtosis=  415.19
```

**Task:** A perfect bell curve scores 0 on both. `AGE` is close to it. `PAY_AMT1` scores a skew of **14.7** and an excess kurtosis of **415**. This is about as far from Normal as tabular data gets. Write down what that means in plain words for someone who has never heard of kurtosis, and name one modelling consequence of a column looking like this.

## Exercise 2.4: Now look at it

Numbers describe; pictures reveal. Plot the two extremes side by side:

```python
fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 3, figsize=(15, 4))

axes[2].set_title("LIMIT_BAL: box plot")
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()
```

**Reflection:** The `PAY_AMT1` histogram is almost useless as drawn: nearly every client sits in the first bar. Try it again with `plt.xscale("log")` (add a small constant first, since **17.5%** of clients paid exactly zero). What does the log-scaled version show you that the raw one cannot? This is why the booklet insists you plot before you trust any number.
