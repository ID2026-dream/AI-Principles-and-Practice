## Exercise 7.2: The datasheet

Where the dictionary describes the columns, a **datasheet** (Gebru et al., 2021) describes the dataset as a whole. Answer these in a paragraph each, for this dataset:

- **Why does it exist, and who made it?** Yeh & Lien (2009), to compare methods for predicting default.
- **Who is represented?** Credit-card clients of *one* bank, in *Taiwan*, over *six months of 2005*. Say plainly who that excludes.
- **How was it collected, and with what consent?** What can you actually establish here, and what can't you?
- **What was done to it before you got it?** Note what the undocumented codes suggest about pre-processing you weren't told about.
- **What may it be used for?** CC BY 4.0. Name one use that would be inappropriate.
- **Known biases and limitations.** This is the important one (see below).

**Step 1:** For that last section, you need a number. Check whether the outcome rate differs across a protected attribute:

```python
# section7_governance.py
g = df.groupby("SEX")["default.payment.next.month"].agg(["mean", "size"])
print(g.assign(mean=g["mean"].round(4)).to_string())
```

```

SEX
```

**Reflection:** Men default at **24.2%**, women at **20.8%**, a 3.4-point gap, present in the data *before any model exists*. No algorithm created it and no algorithm can be blamed for it, but a model trained on this data will learn it and act on it. That belongs in your datasheet's limitations section today, in one sentence, whether or not you know what to do about it.

**This is where the ethics of Weeks 3 and 4 actually begin.** Not in a fairness metric applied to a finished model, but here, in a documented observation about the data. **Week 3** returns to this exact number and asks whether a model that reproduces it is unfair, and what you could do about it.
