## What You'll Learn

Demographic parity ignores who actually defaults. Equalised odds does not: it conditions on the truth. And calibration will set the trap that Section 5 springs.

The equalised-odds difference is about **0.017**, driven by a true-positive-rate gap of roughly **0.02** (male 0.227, female 0.244) with near-identical false-positive rates. Small here, but this is exactly the quantity that a demographic-parity fix will make *worse* in Section 5.

## Exercise 3.2: Calibration by group

A score is calibrated if "0.3" means a 30% default rate, and does so for both groups. Calibration is what makes the impossibility result bite.

```python
# section3_calibration.py
from sklearn.calibration import calibration_curve

plt.figure(figsize=(5, 5))
for g, colour in [("male", "#1f77b4"), ("female", "#ff7f0e")]:
    m = sex == g
    obs, pred = calibration_curve(y_true[m], scores[m], n_bins=5, strategy="quantile")
    plt.plot(pred, obs, "o-", color=colour, label=g)
plt.plot([0, 1], [0, 1], "k--", alpha=0.4, label="perfect")
plt.xlabel("predicted probability of default"); plt.ylabel("observed default rate")
plt.legend(); plt.title("Calibration by group"); plt.tight_layout()
plt.savefig("cal_by_group.png", dpi=130); plt.show()
print("saved cal_by_group.png")
```

The two curves sit close to the diagonal and close to each other: the model is roughly **calibrated within each group**. That sounds like good news. It is about to become the problem.
