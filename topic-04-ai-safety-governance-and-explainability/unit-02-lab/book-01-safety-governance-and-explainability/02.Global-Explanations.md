# Section 2: Explain it globally, importance and SHAP

## What You'll Learn

A global explanation asks how the model behaves across everyone, not why it decided one case. You'll use two global tools, permutation importance and SHAP, and find both that they broadly agree on what matters and that one of them surfaces something uncomfortable the other hides.

## Exercise 2.1: Permutation importance

The honest way to ask "how much does this feature matter" is to shuffle it and measure the damage. If breaking a feature barely moves the score, the model was not really leaning on it.

**Step 1:** Permute each feature and watch the AUC drop:

```python
# section2_permutation.py
from sklearn.inspection import permutation_importance

perm = permutation_importance(logit, Xc_te, yc_te, n_repeats=10,
                              random_state=0, scoring="roc_auc", n_jobs=-1)
cols  = num_c + categorical
order = np.argsort(perm.importances_mean)[::-1]
for i in order[:5]:
    print(f"{cols[i]:11s}  AUC drop = {perm.importances_mean[i]:.4f}")
```

You'll see `PAY_1` tower over everything else, with an AUC drop around **0.113**, while the next feature, `BILL_AMT1`, sits near **0.027**, roughly a quarter as large. Everything else is smaller still.

**Question:** `PAY_1` is the client's repayment status last month. Why would a single "were you late last month" feature dominate a default model, and what does it tell you about how much genuinely independent information the other twenty-two features carry? (This is the correlated-feature problem the lecture warned about: much of the table is telling one story.)

## Exercise 2.2: SHAP, and the feature it surfaces

SHAP splits each prediction fairly across its features using a result from game theory. For a linear model the split has a closed form and is quick to compute.

**Step 1:** Explain the model globally and rank features by their mean absolute SHAP value:

```python
# section2_shap.py
import shap

prep = logit.named_steps["prep"]
clf  = logit.named_steps["clf"]
names = [n.split("__", 1)[1] for n in prep.get_feature_names_out()]
Xtr_t = prep.transform(Xc_tr)
Xte_t = prep.transform(Xc_te)

background = shap.utils.sample(Xtr_t, 100, random_state=0)
explainer  = shap.LinearExplainer(clf, background)
shap_values = explainer.shap_values(Xte_t)

mean_abs = np.abs(shap_values).mean(0)
for i in np.argsort(mean_abs)[::-1][:4]:
    print(f"{names[i]:12s}  mean|SHAP| = {mean_abs[i]:.3f}")
# a beeswarm shows the spread; uncomment to view
# shap.summary_plot(shap_values, Xte_t, feature_names=names, show=True)
```

The top of the ranking is `PAY_1` (about **0.459**) then `BILL_AMT1` (about **0.308**), agreeing with permutation importance. Then comes something the AUC-drop ranking buried: **`SEX_2`**, at about **0.217**, the third strongest driver in the whole model.

**Task:** Two global methods, two different pictures below the top. Permutation importance said `SEX` barely mattered; SHAP puts it third. Write two sentences on why that is not a contradiction. (Hint: permutation measures damage to *AUC* when you scramble a feature; SHAP measures how much the feature *moves individual predictions*. A feature can shift many decisions without changing the ranking much.)

**Reflection:** `SEX` is a protected attribute, and your model is using it as one of its three loudest features. That is not hidden bias to be uncovered later; it is the model, as built, lending differently to men and women.
