# Section 4: Break it on purpose, robustness and silent failure

## What You'll Learn

The dangerous failure is the quiet one: the model keeps returning confident answers while the world moves under it, and the metric on the dashboard keeps smiling. You'll cause that failure deliberately and, more importantly, measure the thing it hides rather than the thing it announces.

## Exercise 4.1: Simulate a downturn

The model was trained on 2005. Imagine a downturn in which repayment gets harder: some clients who were already a month behind, and whom the model approved, now tip into default. The model does not change; the world does.

**Step 1:** Age the world and measure four numbers before and after:

```python
# section4_shift.py
approved = (pred == 0)                 # the model "approves" whoever it predicts won't default
y_true   = yc_te.values
pay1     = Xc_te["PAY_1"].values

# downturn: previously non-defaulting clients already >= 1 month behind now default
y_down = y_true.copy()
y_down[(y_true == 0) & (pay1 >= 1)] = 1

def snapshot(y):
    from sklearn.metrics import accuracy_score
    return dict(default_rate = y.mean(),
                approval_rate = approved.mean(),
                bad_rate_among_approved = y[approved].mean(),
                accuracy = accuracy_score(y, pred))

before, after = snapshot(y_true), snapshot(y_down)
for k in before:
    print(f"{k:26s} {before[k]:.3f} -> {after[k]:.3f}")
```

You'll see the population default rate climb from **0.221 to 0.330**, and the approved book rot with it: the bad-loan rate among approved clients rises from **0.182 to 0.278**. But look at the approval rate: **0.928 before, 0.928 after**, frozen to three decimals, because the model's decisions never changed. The one number an operations dashboard watches in real time has not moved at all.

**Question:** Accuracy here does slip, from 0.812 to 0.742, so why call this *silent*? Because accuracy is not a number you can see live. Computing it needs the realised `default` labels, and in a real lender those arrive months after the loan is approved. Live, the only thing you can watch is the frozen approval rate, and it is blind. By the time the labels let you recompute accuracy, the bad loans are already on the books.

(Think about the *inputs*: the distribution of `PAY_1`, the share of applicants already behind, the drift in incoming feature values against the training distribution.) Two or three sentences.

**Reflection:** The lecture's line was "monitoring is the only thing standing between a silent failure and a headline". You have now seen why it has to be designed *before* deployment: the metric that matters cannot be computed when you need it, so you must instrument its leading indicators in advance. There is no retrofitting this after the book has gone bad.
