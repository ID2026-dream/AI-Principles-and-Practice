# Section 2: K-means, and choosing K by argument

## What You'll Learn

K-means is short to run and quick to misread. The algorithm needs you to fix the number of clusters, `K`, in advance, and there is no true `K` waiting in the data to be discovered. This section runs K-means across a range of `K`, reads two disagreeing instruments, and makes you commit to a number you can defend in a sentence.

## Exercise 2.1: Scan K with the elbow and the silhouette

**Step 1:** For each `K`, fit K-means and print two numbers: the within-cluster inertia (which the elbow reads) and the mean silhouette (which measures separation).

```python
# section2_scan.py
from sklearn.cluster import KMeans
from sklearn.metrics import silhouette_score

for k in range(2, 9):
    km = KMeans(n_clusters=k, n_init=10, random_state=42).fit(Xt)
    sil = silhouette_score(Xt, km.labels_, sample_size=5000, random_state=42)
    print(f"K={k}  inertia={km.inertia_:11.0f}  silhouette={sil:.3f}")
```

You should see inertia fall smoothly, from about `512,700` at `K=2` down to roughly `331,000` at `K=8`, with the steepest single drop between two and three. The silhouette does something more decisive: it is highest at `K=2` (about `0.356`) and then collapses to `0.217` at `K=3` and lower after.

**Question:** The silhouette points clearly at `K=2`. But `0.356` is not a strong silhouette; values near one mean crisp, obvious clusters. What is the honest reading of a *clear* preference for `K=2` at a *weak* absolute height? (For a start: there is one genuinely distinctive group and a large, loose remainder, rather than several clean clusters.)

**Step 2 (optional plot):** See the two curves for yourself.

```python
# section2_plot.py
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

ks = list(range(2, 9))
inertia, sils = [], []
for k in ks:
    km = KMeans(n_clusters=k, n_init=10, random_state=42).fit(Xt)
    inertia.append(km.inertia_)
    sils.append(silhouette_score(Xt, km.labels_, sample_size=5000, random_state=42))

fig, ax = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(10, 4))
ax[0].plot(ks, inertia, "-o"); ax[0].set_title("elbow (inertia)"); ax[0].set_xlabel("K")
ax[1].plot(ks, sils, "-o");    ax[1].set_title("silhouette");     ax[1].set_xlabel("K")
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()
```

## Exercise 2.2: Commit to a K and look at what it gives you

**Step 1:** Fit the `K=2` clustering you argued for, and look at the sizes.

```python
# section2_final.py
km2 = KMeans(n_clusters=2, n_init=10, random_state=42).fit(Xt)
labels = km2.labels_
print(np.bincount(labels))                     # roughly [ 4617 25383 ]
```

The split is lopsided: about `4,600` clients in one cluster and `25,400` in the other. So "two clusters" here does not mean "the population halves neatly". It means the data contains one distinctive minority and a large, undifferentiated majority.

**Step 2:** Find out *who* the small cluster is by comparing a few raw features across the two groups.

```python
# section2_who.py
small = 0 if (labels == 0).sum() < (labels == 1).sum() else 1
for col in ["LIMIT_BAL", "BILL_AMT1", "PAY_AMT1"]:
    hi = df[col][labels == small].mean()
    lo = df[col][labels != small].mean()
    print(f"{col:11s}  small cluster={hi:11.0f}   rest={lo:11.0f}")
```

The small cluster is the high-balance clients: their average latest bill is around `180,000` against about `28,000` for everyone else, with correspondingly larger credit limits and payments. That is a real, interpretable segment, big spenders carrying large balances, not a statistical accident.

**Reflection:** You now have a clustering, and it is not nonsense: it found the big-balance clients. But go back to the sentence you wrote in Exercise 1.2 about what a *good* clustering would be. Does "separates the big spenders from everyone else" meet it? For a bank trying to understand *default risk*, maybe not at all, and Section 5 will show you why. A clustering can be perfectly real and still answer a question you did not ask.
