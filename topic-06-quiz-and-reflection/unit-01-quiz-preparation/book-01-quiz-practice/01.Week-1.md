# Week 1: Data Analysis and the ML Workflow

> **If you remember one thing.** Look before you trust, and split before you learn. A single number can hide the shape of the data, so always plot. And anything learned from the whole dataset before the train/test split leaks the test set into training, which manufactures accuracy out of nothing.

## The points

### 1.1 The workflow is a loop

Every project moves through eight stages: **frame** the problem, **collect** data, **explore** it (EDA, exploratory data analysis), **preprocess** it, **model**, **evaluate**, **deploy** and **monitor**. It is a **loop, not a line**: what evaluation and monitoring reveal sends you back to reframe and to better data, because real-world data **drifts** and a model that was good last year quietly goes stale. Weeks 1 to 5 live almost entirely in the first four stages, which is where most projects are won or lost.

### 1.2 Know your variable types

Five kinds. **Binary**: two values (churn). **Categorical**, or nominal: named groups with no order (region, plan). **Ordinal**: named groups with an order (satisfaction). **Discrete**: whole-number counts (tenure in months). **Continuous**: any value on a scale (age, income). The type decides the summary statistic, the plot, the encoding and the model. Models only eat numbers, so words must be encoded. **Ordinal encoding** maps Low, Medium, High to 0, 1, 2, and only works if you **state the order yourself**: left alone, the encoder sorts alphabetically and puts Low between High and Medium. **One-hot encoding** gives a nominal column one yes-or-no column per category, so no false order is invented; it becomes wasteful when a column has thousands of values, such as a postcode.

### 1.3 Centre, spread and shape

**Centre**: the **mean** chases outliers; the **median** does not. In the running example the mean income is 53,565 and the median 40,388, about 32% higher: the signature of a right-skewed column with a few huge values (the billionaire walking into the room). The median is the honest typical value. **Spread**: the standard deviation (112,607) is inflated by the same outliers; the **IQR**, Q3 minus Q1, covers the middle half of the data and ignores the extremes (23,205). Sample variance divides by n − 1. **Shape**: skewness measures lopsidedness and kurtosis measures heavy tails. In a **box plot** the box is the IQR, the line is the median, the whiskers reach 1.5 IQRs, and points beyond them are flagged.

### 1.4 Always plot

The Normal (bell) curve is fully described by its mean and standard deviation, but most real data is not Normal. Two columns can share **the same mean and the same standard deviation** and still be different shapes, one with a single hump and one with two. The numbers cannot tell them apart; a histogram shows it in a second. Choose the chart for the question: histogram, density or box plot for one column; scatter plot for two; grouped bars or a box plot per group to compare groups; a line chart over time; a heatmap or pair plot for many columns. Label honestly.

### 1.5 Correlation and its traps

**Pearson** measures **straight-line** association, from −1 to 1, and a few extreme values can wreck it. **Spearman** correlates the **ranks**, so it asks whether two things rise together at all (monotonic), and it is immune to outliers and curves. On the same two columns, income and monthly charge, Pearson said 0.14 and Spearman 0.82. **Anscombe’s quartet**: four datasets with identical mean, variance, correlation and regression line, and completely different pictures. And **correlation is not causation**: ice-cream sales and drownings rise together because hot weather drives both. Ask whether one causes the other, whether a third thing causes both, or whether it is chance in a small sample.

### 1.6 How the data arrives, and one ruler for every column

**Batch**: a fixed dataset processed together; reproducible and easy to check, the default for training and coursework. **Stream**: data keeps arriving, memory is bounded, statistics update as you go, and you must watch for **drift**. Decide which you are in before you build. **Scaling**: models that use distances or gradient steps need comparable scales, or income in tens of thousands drowns out age in tens purely because of its units. The **z-score** subtracts the mean and divides by the standard deviation; **min–max** squeezes a column into the range 0 to 1.

### 1.7 Missing data: the mechanism matters

**MCAR**, missing completely at random: the gap has nothing to do with anything, like a sensor dropping a reading; dropping those rows is unbiased, just wasteful. **MAR**, missing at random: the gap is explained by something you did record (younger users skip the income question), so the other columns can fill it. **MNAR**, missing not at random: the gap is explained by the missing value itself (high earners hide their income); no simple fill is fully honest. Consider a **was-missing flag**, because refusing to answer can itself predict behaviour. **Median imputation** is robust but stacks every gap on one value, creating a spike and shrinking the spread; KNN imputation borrows from similar rows. Missingness can also hide in plain sight: in the lab the credit file had zero blanks, yet undocumented category codes were unknown values in disguise.

### 1.8 Outliers: flag, then decide

Two rules. The **z-score rule** flags anything more than 3 standard deviations from the mean, and assumes a roughly Normal column. The **IQR rule** flags anything below Q1 − 1.5 × IQR or above Q3 + 1.5 × IQR (the box plot’s whiskers) and assumes nothing about shape, so it is the safer default for messy data. In the example it drew the line at about 88,000 and flagged 26 incomes. **Flagging is not deleting.** Decide by cause: fix an obvious typo, keep a rare-but-real value (perhaps treating it separately), recognise a different population, and never delete a value just because it is inconvenient.

### 1.9 Too little data, and augmentation

With too few examples a model **memorises** the rows it saw (overfitting). Rare classes vanish: only 91 of 600 customers churned, so predicting “stayed” for everyone scores about 85% accuracy and catches no one. The menu, best first: collect more real data; reuse a pre-trained model (transfer learning); a simpler, regularised model; and **augmentation**, which manufactures extra training rows using **label-preserving** transformations. That one rule is the whole game: flip a photo of a 6 upside down and it becomes a 9, which is corruption, not augmentation. **SMOTE** places a new minority row a random fraction of the way from a real minority point to one of its near minority neighbours; **jitter** adds small noise scaled to the column’s spread; generative models (VAEs, GANs, diffusion) learn the distribution and sample from it. Augmentation cannot invent a population your data never contained, and generators can amplify bias that is already there.

### 1.10 Leakage, and the pipeline that prevents it

**Leakage**: any statistic learned from the whole dataset **before** the train/test split (scaling, imputing, encoding, feature selection, augmentation) lets the test set seep into training. The demonstration: 200 rows, 5,000 columns of pure noise, random labels, so there is nothing to learn. Choose the 20 best-looking columns using all the data and cross-validation reports 0.795; put the selection **inside the pipeline** and it reports 0.510, chance, as it must. The cure is mechanical: **split first**, fit every transform on the training data only, wrap the whole sequence in one **Pipeline** (with a ColumnTransformer for the columns), and cross-validate the pipeline. Augment only the training split, after the split; never the test set.

### 1.11 Documentation is governance

A **data dictionary** explains every column: its type, units and quirks (income: continuous, euros, skewed, 8.8% missing). A **datasheet** explains the whole dataset: why and how it was collected, by whom and under what consent, who is represented and **who is missing**, the licence, and known biases and limits. This is where the ethics of Weeks 3 and 4 begins, because most unfairness is inherited from the data, not invented by the algorithm. Write it **before** modelling: a datasheet written first is a description; one written afterwards is a justification.

## The nine questions to ask of any dataset

You may be asked to apply these, so read each one as a test you could run.

| # | Question |
|---|---|
| 1 | What decision is this for, and which stage of the workflow am I in? |
| 2 | What type is each column, and does its encoding respect whether order is real? |
| 3 | Is the column skewed enough that the median, not the mean, is the honest typical value? |
| 4 | Have I plotted it, or am I trusting a summary number? |
| 5 | Is the association linear or only monotonic, and could a third variable explain it? |
| 6 | Why is this value missing: at random, explained by another column, or by the value itself? |
| 7 | Is this outlier an error, rare but real, or a different population? |
| 8 | Did I split before learning anything from the data, with every step inside one pipeline? |
| 9 | Who is missing from this data, and have I written that down? |

## Twenty practice questions

Multiple choice, one right answer each. Answers are in the **Answers** step at the end of this lab.

**1. Two columns have exactly the same mean and the same standard deviation. What can you conclude about their shapes?**

- A. They share a distribution, because centre and spread fully define one
- B. Both are Normal, since a mean and a standard deviation describe them
- C. They must be strongly correlated, since their summaries are identical
- D. Nothing yet: their shapes could differ, so you must plot them

**2. Only 15% of customers churn. A churn model scores 85% accuracy. What should worry you?**

- A. Nothing: 85% accuracy is a strong result for customer data
- B. Predicting “stayed” for everyone also scores about 85%
- C. The model is underfitting and needs more features before it is useful
- D. 85% is too high to be genuine for churn, so it must be leakage

**3. Choosing the 20 best of 5,000 noise columns using all the data, then cross-validating, gives 0.795. With the selection inside a pipeline it gives 0.510. Which number is honest?**

- A. 0.795, because it used all of the data to choose the best features
- B. 0.510, because selection was refit inside each training fold
- C. Both, because cross-validation was used to produce each of the numbers
- D. Neither, because 5,000 columns is far too many for any model to handle

**4. Why does Week 1 insist that the datasheet be written before modelling?**

- A. Regulators require the datasheet to be dated earlier than the model
- B. A model cannot be trained unless a datasheet file is already present
- C. The datasheet records the model’s final accuracy, so it must come first
- D. Written first it describes the data; written later it justifies the model

**5. A model measures distances between customers using age (18 to 85) and income (tens of thousands), and neither column was scaled. What happens?**

- A. Age dominates, because it has far fewer distinct values than income
- B. Income drowns out age purely because its numbers are much larger
- C. Nothing, because distance-based models ignore each column’s units
- D. The model rescales both columns itself before it measures distance

**6. A team applies SMOTE to the whole dataset and then splits it into training and test sets. What is wrong?**

- A. Synthetic rows built from test rows can leak into training
- B. Nothing is wrong, provided the random seed is fixed for reproducibility
- C. SMOTE belongs on the test set only, so that the test classes are balanced
- D. SMOTE should run after training, as a check on the finished model

**7. Four datasets have identical means, variances, correlations and regression lines. What lesson do they teach?**

- A. Summary statistics can hide very different data, so always plot
- B. The four datasets must have been drawn from one underlying source
- C. Linear regression is the right model for all four, as the lines agree
- D. Correlation fully describes a relationship once the variances match

**8. A survey records satisfaction as Low, Medium or High. A colleague encodes it with an ordinal encoder without passing a category order. What goes wrong?**

- A. Nothing: an ordinal encoder reads the natural order from the words

- C. It makes three yes-or-no columns, because no order was given to it

**9. A column is heavily right-skewed with a few extreme values. Which measure of spread should you report, and why?**

- A. The standard deviation, because it is in the data’s own units
- B. The variance, because squaring the distances cancels the outliers out
- C. The range, because it is the only measure that shows the full extent
- D. The IQR, because it covers the middle half and ignores the extremes

**10. A team augments a handwritten-digit dataset by rotating every image by 180 degrees. Why is that a bug?**

**11. After filling every missing income with the median, a histogram shows a tall spike at one value and the standard deviation has fallen. What happened?**

- A. The imputer was fitted on the wrong column and simply needs rerunning
- B. The data already held a large cluster of customers at the median
- C. Every gap was filled with one value, distorting the shape and spread
- D. Nothing is wrong: imputation always leaves a distribution more accurate

**12. Customers with high incomes are more likely to leave the income question blank. Which missingness mechanism is this?**
