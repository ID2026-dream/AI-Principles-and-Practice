# Week 2: Supervised Learning

> **If you remember one thing.** The question sets the target, the target sets the metric, and the metric sets the threshold. Accuracy is the number that flatters most, and on rare events it is worthless; decide which error hurts more before you choose how to measure.

## The points

### 2.1 Generalisation, and the three splits

A supervised model is a function: features x in, a prediction ŷ out, ŷ = f(x; θ); training tunes the parameters θ. **Fitting the training data is easy; the goal is generalisation**, being right about the next case. Memorising is not learning; it only looks like it until the test set. Hold data back in **three** slices: **training** fits the parameters; **validation** chooses everything else (which model, which degree, which k, how much regularisation); **test** is spent **once**, at the very end. Every time you peek at the test set to make a decision, it stops being a test.

### 2.2 The target is a decision

The quick answer: a number on a scale means **regression**; a category means **classification**. The better answer: the **target is a decision you make, not a fact of the data**. On one customers table, “how much will they spend?” (monthly charge, regression) and “will they leave at all?” (churn, classification) are different problems. Choosing the target is already modelling: get it wrong and the cleverest algorithm answers a question nobody asked.

### 2.3 Linear regression, read honestly

Linear regression chooses coefficients to make the total squared error as small as possible; least squares has a closed form, so it is fast. Each coefficient is the effect of one feature **holding the rest fixed**, and its sign and size are a **claim about the world**: a surprising sign means either the world or your data is strange. It is the **honest baseline**: beat it before reaching for anything fancier. **R²** is the share of variation explained (0.67 on monthly charge). Always read the **residuals** as well: a high R² with a curved or funnel-shaped residual plot is a warning, not a win.

### 2.4 Capacity, and regularisation

Polynomial features (x, x², x³ and so on) let the same linear machinery bend, and the degree is a **capacity dial**. In the booklet: degree 1 underfits (training R² 0.53, test 0.51); degree 3 is about right (0.79 and 0.70); degree 15 overfits (training up to 0.82, test **down** to 0.65). **Regularisation** charges for complexity with a penalty λ. **Ridge** penalises squared coefficients and shrinks them all smoothly, which suits correlated, noisy features. **Lasso** penalises absolute values and can push weak coefficients **exactly to zero**, so it also **selects** features. On a few clean features it barely matters (R² 0.668 against 0.670); on hundreds of correlated ones it is the difference between a model and a memoriser.

### 2.5 Three classifiers and their dials

**Logistic regression** is a classifier despite its name: a linear score squashed by the sigmoid into a **probability**, and a threshold, which you choose, turns that into a decision. **k-NN** does no training: it votes among the k nearest points. k is the dial (k = 1 memorises; a very large k smooths into mush), and because it rests on distance it **breaks without scaling**. **Decision trees** ask one yes/no question at a time, each split making the groups purer (Gini or entropy). They are the most readable model here and among the easiest to overfit; depth is the dial. **Interpretable and reliable are different virtues.**

### 2.6 Ensembles and support vector machines

**Bagging**, as in a random forest, grows many trees on resampled data and different feature subsets; each is wrong in its own direction and averaging cancels the noise, so **variance falls**. **Boosting** adds small trees in sequence, each fixing the last one’s mistakes, so **bias falls**, but greed can overfit. Ensembles win by **diversity, not brilliance**. A **support vector machine** finds the boundary with the **widest margin**; only the points on the edge, the **support vectors**, shape it; C trades margin width against tolerating awkward points; the **kernel trick** gives a curved boundary. Reach for it on modest data or when features outnumber rows; it gives distances, not calibrated probabilities.

### 2.7 Loss: what you optimise is what you get

**MSE**, squared error, punishes big misses hardest. **MAE**, the plain average error, stops a few outliers dominating the fit. **Log-loss**, for classification, punishes **confident wrong answers**. The loss encodes your priorities before a single parameter is learned, so “which loss?” really means “which mistakes can I least afford?”. Answer that first; the optimisation just obeys.

### 2.8 Bias, variance and learning curves

**Underfitting**: too rigid; training and validation scores both poor and close together; the cure is more capacity. **Overfitting**: too flexible; brilliant on training data, poor on new data, with a wide gap; the cure is less capacity or more data. **Same symptom, opposite cures**, so diagnose before you act. Expected error is bias² plus variance plus irreducible noise; no model beats the noise, and the job is the bottom of the U, not zero bias or zero variance. Read a **learning curve** (score against training-set size): **a gap that will not close** means variance, so add data, simplify or regularise; **both curves flat and low** means bias, so add features or capacity, and more data will not help.

### 2.9 Cross-validation, and regression metrics

One split gives one number and a false sense of precision; it is an anecdote. **k-fold cross-validation** rotates the held-out slice so every point is judged once, giving a **mean and a spread**. Cross-validate the **whole pipeline**, so the scaler and imputer are refit inside each fold, or you are back to Week 1’s leakage. **RMSE** is the typical error in the target’s own units and punishes large misses; **MAE** is the plain average error; **R²** is unitless and comparable but flatters on easy data. When RMSE is much larger than MAE, a few big errors are doing the damage.

### 2.10 Accuracy lies; precision and recall tell you more

On rare events accuracy is the number that lies most: with 3.4% fraud, “never fraud” scores 96.6% and catches nothing. The churn models all sat near 88% accuracy while recall ran from 0.39 (k-NN, missing six in ten leavers) to 0.78; in the lab every model scored about 81% while logistic regression caught only 24% of defaulters. The **confusion matrix** shows four outcomes, and the two errors **rarely cost the same**: decide which hurts more first. **Precision** = TP ÷ (TP + FP): of those flagged, how many were real. **Recall** = TP ÷ (TP + FN): of the real ones, how many were caught. Push one up and the other usually falls. **F1** splits the difference, which only makes sense if both errors cost the same.

### 2.11 ROC, precision–recall, and the threshold

The **ROC** curve sweeps every threshold; **AUC** is the chance the model ranks a random positive above a random negative, and 0.5 is a coin toss. It is threshold-free and good for ranking, but says nothing about **where to cut**. With **rare positives** the ROC curve looks reassuringly high even for a nearly useless model; the **precision–recall** curve ignores the huge, easy negative class and stays honest, so read **PR-AUC**. The **threshold is a dial, not a default**: 0.5 is a convention. Set it where the costs of the two errors balance. In the lab, moving it from 0.5 to 0.2 raised recall from 0.24 to about 0.68 while precision fell from 0.73 to about 0.32. It is a business decision wearing a maths costume.

### 2.12 Framing real problems

The chain is **question, then target, then metric, then threshold**: what decision, predict what, measured how, act when. The algorithm is the easy, replaceable part; get the target and metric right and a plain logistic regression beats a mis-aimed neural network. The same algorithms serve every domain, but the metric changes: **medical diagnosis** puts **recall** first, because a missed case is the costly mistake; **spam and fraud** need **precision** and PR-AUC, because a blocked genuine card is a furious customer. A probability is not an action: the model informs and the **policy decides**. And data drifts, so last year’s threshold quietly rots.

## The five questions before you choose a target and metric

This is the lecture’s own checklist. You may be asked to apply it, so read each one as a test you could run. Answer all five and you have done the hard 80% of the modelling before importing a single estimator.

| # | Question |
|---|---|
| 1 | What decision does this feed, and what does a wrong one cost? |
| 2 | Is the honest target a number or a category? |
| 3 | Which error, a false positive or a false negative, hurts more here? |
| 4 | Is the positive class rare? If so, retire accuracy and ROC for precision–recall. |
| 5 | Where should the threshold sit so the costs actually balance? |
