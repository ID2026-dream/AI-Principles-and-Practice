# Week 3: Ethics, Bias and Fairness

> **If you remember one thing.** “Fair” means nothing without a criterion. One model can fail three fairness tests and pass a fourth, and when groups have different base rates the criteria cannot all hold at once. Every fix moves unfairness rather than deleting it, so choose openly, cost the choice, and name who pays.

## The points

### 3.1 Fairness is a modelling concern

If ethics comes last it is decoration: by the time the model ships, every consequential choice (what to predict, about whom, optimised for what) is frozen. Fairness is decided **upstream**, at the **target**, the **loss** and the **data**. A model is an **argument about the world**: the target says what counts as success, the loss says which mistakes you will accept, and the data says whose past you generalise from. None is neutral. So do not ask “is this model biased?” as a yes or no; ask “**what does it assume, and who benefits if the assumption holds?**”

### 3.2 Accuracy is already a value choice

Accuracy treats every error the same; reality never does. A model that is 95% accurate can be catastrophic if the 5% it fails are all the same people, and the average hides that. Three **ethical lenses** judge the same threshold differently. **Consequentialist**: does it produce better outcomes overall? It ignores how the harm is distributed. **Deontological**: does it treat people as ends, by fair rules? It may reject a model that helps the average person. **Rawlsian justice**: does it improve the position of the worst-off? It can cost aggregate performance. Pick the lens **before** you tune, or the metric picks the ethics for you.

### 3.3 Whose fairness, and why it is close to zero-sum

The applicant wants an equal shot, the lender wants precision on repayment, the regulator wants no disparate impact, and society wants the historical gap to shrink. Some of these are mutually exclusive, and **whoever defines fairness is usually not the person the model decides about**. The better question is not “how accurate is it?” but “could you **defend** this decision to the person it was made about?” With a bounded budget (one pool of approvals, one threshold), an assumption that fits one group well and another badly **allocates** approvals between them. It is not strictly zero-sum: better data or features can lift both groups, but that runs out where base rates genuinely differ.

### 3.4 Three meanings of “bias”

**Statistical** bias: an estimator systematically off the true value; fixed with more or better data or a better model. **Social** bias: systematically worse outcomes for a group; the fix is sometimes structural, not technical. **Cognitive** bias: human judgement skewed by mental shortcuts; fixed with process, review and diverse teams. Keep them apart, because **reducing statistical bias can increase social bias**: a more accurate model of an unjust world reproduces the injustice more faithfully.

### 3.5 Data bias, and the label

The training set is a record, not a neutral photograph. Four kinds: **sampling** (who is over- or under-represented), **historical** (past decisions baked in as ground truth), **measurement** (the proxy you could record is not the thing you meant) and **labelling** (someone’s judgement frozen as the target). The **label** is usually the real crime scene. You wanted creditworthiness; what you have is “did the bank approve them last time, and did they repay”, shaped by who was offered credit at all. Train on that and you learn **who the old system favoured**. Audit the gap between a proxy label and what you care about before you touch a metric.

### 3.6 Algorithmic and societal bias: point at the stage

Even with clean data the algorithm adds a slant: the **objective** rewards shortcuts you did not mean; **aggregation** optimises the average, a choice about whose errors are visible; **regularisation** nudges toward the majority pattern and smooths minorities away. The optimiser is faithful, not malicious. **Societal** bias: some gaps are real because the world that produced them was unequal, and a model can be perfectly accurate about that world and still entrench it. **Accuracy and justice are different axes.** When someone says “the algorithm is biased”, make them point at the stage; usually it is **upstream** of the model.

### 3.7 Deleting the column does not work, and feedback makes it worse

**Fairness through unawareness** (deleting the sensitive column) is a weak defence. Through **redundant encoding**, other features act as **proxies**: in the lecture’s credit model, group membership was recoverable from the score alone **70%** of the time, against a **42%** base rate. Blindness is not fairness. **Feedback loops**: deny loans to a group and you never observe their repayments, so next year’s data holds even less signal about them, and the model grows more confident and more wrong. A snapshot audit misses this. In the lab, on the real credit data, sex was **not** recoverable from the other features: you do not know until you measure.

### 3.8 One confusion matrix per group

Every group fairness metric compares **two confusion matrices**, one per group: compute Week 2’s matrix for each group, then argue about which cell you allow to differ. **Group fairness** compares rates across groups: easy to measure, blind to the individual. **Individual fairness**: similar people, similar treatment; intuitive, but defining “similar” smuggles values back in. Enforcing one can violate the other.

### 3.9 Demographic parity and the four-fifths rule

**Demographic parity**: the same approval rate in every group. Simple and legible, but it looks only at the decision, never at who deserved it; if outcomes genuinely differ, it can force approving less-qualified applicants. It is right when you believe the **label itself is corrupted** by past injustice, and that is an argument you must make. **Disparate impact** is a legal test in **ratio space**: the disadvantaged group’s selection rate divided by the advantaged group’s must reach **0.80**, the **four-fifths rule**. The lecture model approves 58% against 22%: 0.215 ÷ 0.577 = **0.37**, under half the floor.

### 3.10 Equalised odds, calibration, and four verdicts

**Equalised odds**: equal true-positive and equal false-positive rates across groups, so being right or wrong does not depend on your group. It **conditions on the truth** and is often the more defensible ask. **Equal opportunity** relaxes it to equal true-positive rates only (“don’t miss the deserving”). The lecture model: TPR 75% against 39%. **Calibration**: a score of 0.7 means a 70% rate in every group. Here precision is nearly level (69% against 66%) and the model is roughly calibrated, while selection and TPR are miles apart. One model fails parity, disparate impact and equal opportunity, and passes calibration. The real question: “**fair by which criterion**, and can you justify that choice?”

### 3.11 The impossibility result

When two groups have **different base rates**, no classifier can have equal calibration, equal false-positive rates and equal false-negative rates at once, except in degenerate cases (Kleinberg, Mullainathan and Raghavan, 2016; Chouldechova, 2017). It is a theorem, not a lack of effort. Watch it happen: force equal selection and Group B’s precision falls from 66% to about 50%. You **moved the unfairness; you did not remove it**. So a tool that promises a “fair” model with no further questions has made the choice for you. Choosing a criterion is a **stance** on whether the base-rate gap is real or the residue of injustice; that belongs to stakeholders and the affected public, and your job is to make it legible and priced.

### 3.12 Mitigation, who pays, and the assessment

Three places to intervene. **Pre-processing** changes the data (reweight, relabel, resample) and touches the truth. **In-processing** puts fairness into training as a constraint: most principled, least portable. **Post-processing** sets per-group thresholds: easiest to bolt on, explicitly unequal treatment, so be ready to say so publicly. **Cost the fix and name who pays**: pushing toward parity barely moved aggregate accuracy, but Group A’s approvals fell from 58% to 45%. **Mitigation is redistribution.** The one-page **ethical-impact assessment** has five parts: Context; Findings, worst gap first; Choice, which criterion and why; Cost, what it spent and who bore it; Residual risk, what you did not fix and what would make you refuse to ship.

## The eight questions for any claim that a model is fair

You may be asked to apply these, so read each one as a test you could run.

| # | Question |
|---|---|
| 1 | At which stage of the pipeline did the bias enter? Point at it. |
| 2 | Is the label the thing you care about, or a record of what an old system decided? |
| 3 | Which ethical lens did you choose, and did you choose it before tuning? |
| 4 | Fair by which criterion, and could you defend that choice to the person decided about? |
| 5 | Do the groups’ base rates differ? If so, which criteria cannot hold together? |
| 6 | Did you measure whether the removed attribute can be recovered, or assume it? |
| 7 | What does the mitigation cost, and who pays? |
| 8 | What would make you refuse to ship? |
