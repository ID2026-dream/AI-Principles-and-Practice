# Week 4: AI Safety, Governance and Explainability

> **If you remember one thing.** The dangerous failure is silent: the model runs as specified, the dashboard stays green, and it harms someone anyway. Governance is whether an outsider could reconstruct who decided what and why. And an explanation makes a model interrogable, not trustworthy.

## The points

### 4.1 A safety failure is not a crash

A **bug** makes the program stop: visible, local and contained, and your test suite is built to catch it. A **safety failure** is the program **running perfectly, doing exactly what you specified, and harming someone anyway**: a clean answer that is confidently wrong, for reasons your tests never sampled. “It passed all tests” is not a safety claim, because tests check the cases you thought of and safety is about the ones you did not.

### 4.2 Specification and alignment

You never hand a model your intent; you hand it a **proxy**: a target column, a loss function, a dataset. **Alignment** is how faithfully that proxy tracks what you meant. Intent: approve applicants who will repay. Specification: maximise accuracy on “did the old system approve them, and did they repay”. The gap is everything the second quietly assumes about the first: that past decisions were fair, that everyone who could have repaid got the chance, that the past predicts the future. Every specification is a translation, so ask what got lost, and whether that loss can hurt a person.

### 4.3 Specification gaming and reward hacking

Give a system a goal it can reach the cheap way and it will, not from malice but because you left the cheap way open. **Specification gaming**: satisfying the letter of the objective by an unintended route, like the boat-racing agent that circles a lagoon collecting points and never finishes. Told to predict repayment, the credit model learns to predict **who was approved before**, because that is what the label records. **Reward hacking** is the sharper cousin: a shortcut that scores well and means nothing (optimise clicks, get clickbait). The test: name the cheapest way to satisfy your objective that you would be embarrassed to defend out loud. That sentence is your reward-hacking risk.

### 4.4 Distribution shift and silent failure

A model is a photograph of one moment deployed into a film that keeps rolling, so **distribution shift** is the default, not an edge case. **Covariate shift**: the inputs move (incomes fall, new people apply). **Concept shift**: the relationship moves (the same income now means a lower chance of repaying). **Robustness** is resistance to shift. The dangerous failure is **silent**: in the downturn example accuracy went from 0.70 to 0.71 and the approval rate stayed at 0.42, while the bad-loan rate among approved applicants jumped from 0.32 to 0.51, with no error raised. In the lab the approval rate froze at 0.928 while the bad-loan rate rose from 0.182 to 0.278; accuracy needs default labels that arrive months later, so it cannot warn you live. Watch **leading indicators** such as input drift, and design monitoring **before** deployment.

### 4.5 Capability is not safety; name the mechanism

**Capability** is what a model can do (accuracy, benchmark score). **Safety** is what it can be trusted to do and not do (failure on the unseen, behaviour under stress). A more capable model deserves **more** scrutiny, because its rare confident mistakes become more expensive. Safety is decided across the lifecycle (design, data, training, evaluation, deployment, retirement), and each dial was set by a person; “the model did it” is never an answer. Name the mechanism: strong offline metrics but falling real-world results is **silent failure under shift**; acing the objective through one convenient feature is **specification gaming**; fine on tested inputs and bizarre just outside them is **brittleness**; fair by the metric but harming one group is a **proxy label** doing unseen work.

### 4.6 Governance is accountability you can audit

Governance answers one question: when this model harms someone, **who is responsible, and how do they show they were careful?** The test: could an outsider, handed your records, **reconstruct who decided what and why**? If not, you have documents, not governance. The **audit trail**: **data provenance** (where each row came from, by what right), **model lineage** (which data, code and version made this prediction) and **decision logs** (what the model output, and who acted on it). All three are **written in advance or not at all**. Governance is a design constraint, not a final gate: retrofitting provenance onto a shipped system costs about ten times as much and convinces nobody.

### 4.7 GDPR: the individual’s leverage

GDPR is not an AI law, but it gives the person the model decides about enforceable rights. **Article 22**: a right not to be subject to a decision based **solely** on automated processing that has a legal or similarly significant effect. “Solely” carries the weight: a human **meaningfully** in the loop, one who can actually disagree, changes the analysis. **Data minimisation**: justify each item of personal data rather than hoarding features “just in case”. The **right to explanation** is legally contested, but the direction of travel is towards more, not less. A credit decision about a named person sits at the **centre** of Article 22, not its edge.

### 4.8 The EU AI Act, NIST, and three philosophies

The **EU AI Act** sorts by **harm, not technology**, in four tiers: **unacceptable** (banned, such as government social scoring), **high-risk** (heavy obligations), **limited** (transparency duties, such as disclosing a bot) and **minimal** (left alone). Creditworthiness assessment is listed in **Annex III**, so it is **high-risk**: a to-do list carried **before** deployment, covering risk management, data governance, technical documentation, record-keeping, human oversight, demonstrated accuracy and robustness, and transparency. The **burden of proof flips**: you must show it is fit before it runs. The **NIST AI RMF** is voluntary and US in origin: **govern, map, measure, manage**, run as a **loop**. GDPR empowers the data subject; the Act, the regulator and the public; NIST, the organisation. They can pull apart (collect less, yet document more). Satisfy the binding instruments and borrow structure from the voluntary one, without treating it as law.

### 4.9 Model cards, datasheets and system cards

**Model card** (Mitchell et al., 2019): a short document that travels with the model: intended use, **out-of-scope** use, training and evaluation data, metrics **disaggregated by group**, ethical considerations and **caveats**. A single accuracy figure is a slogan; the honesty lives in the caveats. “**None identified**” is not a finding; it is a confession that nobody looked. **Datasheet** (Gebru et al.): why the data was collected and by whom, who is in it and **who is missing**, how and by whose judgement it was labelled, and which uses would be inappropriate. Most model failures are dataset failures in disguise. **System card**: the model does not deny a loan, a **system** does; the threshold is a policy choice, and a human may or may not be able to override it. The harm lives in the system, so the governance must too.

### 4.10 Pick your question before your tool

“Why” hides two questions: **global** (how does the model behave across everyone?) and **local** (why this decision, for this person?). A global ranking cannot tell someone why they were refused, and a local story cannot tell you how the population is treated. Two kinds of answer: **intrinsic** (readable by construction, like a shallow tree or a small linear model) and **post-hoc** (fitted onto a black box afterwards). A post-hoc explanation is a **model of a model**: it can be wrong and rarely says so. **Feature importance** misleads quietly: **impurity** importance is biased towards features with many distinct values; **permutation** importance (shuffle a feature, measure the damage) splits credit unpredictably between **correlated** features. Read it as a hint, never as a ranking you would defend in a tribunal.

### 4.11 LIME and SHAP

**LIME** explains one decision: perturb the inputs around one person, watch how the black box responds, and fit a simple linear model to that neighbourhood. It is model-agnostic, intuitive and per-decision, but the **neighbourhood is a choice**, so two analysts with different settings can get different, equally confident explanations of the same refusal. **SHAP** uses **Shapley values** from game theory: each feature is a player, and the prediction is split by average marginal contribution over all orderings. Its guarantees: contributions are **additive** (they sum exactly to the prediction) and **consistent** (a feature that never helps gets no credit). For a linear model each feature’s SHAP value is its weight times its centred value. The guarantees hold **for the model, not the truth**: SHAP shows how the model uses a feature, not whether it deserved to be used.

### 4.12 A faithful explanation is not an exoneration

In the lecture’s model SHAP honestly says “income drove this denial”, but income was built partly from group membership, which was recoverable from the score 70% of the time: income is a **proxy**. The injustice lies in what a feature **is**, not how it is weighed. In the lab SHAP put the protected attribute SEX among the model’s three strongest drivers, in plain sight. Explanations mislead through **instability** (re-run LIME, get a different story), **correlation** (credit flips between correlated features), **plausibility bias** (neat feels true), the **alibi** (lending a bad decision the look of reason) and **surrogate infidelity** (always report fidelity; a 70% surrogate is a confident lie). An explanation makes a model **interrogable, not trustworthy**. Attack it before you quote it.

## The nine questions before you sign a model off

You may be asked to apply these, so read each one as a test you could run.

| # | Question |
|---|---|
| 1 | What is the cheapest way to satisfy the objective that you would be embarrassed to defend out loud? |
| 2 | Which shift would break it, and what would you watch live, before the labels arrive? |
| 3 | Could an outsider, handed your records, reconstruct who decided what and why? |
| 4 | Which EU AI Act tier is it in, and which obligations does that trigger before it runs? |
| 5 | Is a human meaningfully in the loop, or is the decision solely automated? |
| 6 | Which “why” is being asked, global or local, and does the explanation answer that one? |
| 7 | Have you attacked the explanation (seed, neighbourhood, correlated features) before quoting it? |
| 8 | Is the top feature a cause, or a proxy for something the model was never given? |
| 9 | What would you refuse to deploy without? |
