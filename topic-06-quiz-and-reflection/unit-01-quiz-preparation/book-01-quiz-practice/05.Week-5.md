# Week 5: Unsupervised Learning and the Idea of a Representation

> **If you remember one thing.** A representation is a learned description of your data, and it is never neutral. Clustering proposes structure rather than discovering it, so every K is a decision you defend; and a label-free PCA axis rebuilt the deleted group 71% of the time, which no silhouette would ever have flagged.

## The points

### 5.1 No labels means no answer key

**Supervised** learning hands you inputs with correct answers and asks you to reproduce them on new data; success has a definition and a test set to measure it. **Unsupervised** learning hands you inputs only and asks you to find structure: groups, directions, a lower-dimensional shape. The label did a lot of quiet work: it gave you accuracy, a stopping rule, a way to compare models and a way to spot overfitting. Remove it and all of that leaves with it. There is **no answer key**, so nothing can mark your output right or wrong.

### 5.2 Clustering proposes structure; it does not discover it

Unlearn the phrase “it found the groups that are really there”. A clustering algorithm **imposes** a structure you chose (so many groups, of an assumed shape) and reports how well the data tolerated it. The output is a **hypothesis** you proposed, and the burden is on you to argue it is useful. Supervised learning asks “can I reproduce the known answer?”; unsupervised learning asks “**is this structure worth believing?**”, which no single number can settle. Running K-means is one line; deciding whether its output means anything is the actual skill.

### 5.3 K-means, and what it really minimises

You fix **K** in advance. Then **Lloyd’s algorithm** repeats two steps until nothing moves: **assign** each point to its nearest centre (the centroid) by straight-line distance, then **update** each centroid to the mean of its points. The starting centres are random, so results can vary; scikit-learn’s n_init runs it several times and keeps the best. The loop is descending the **within-cluster inertia**: the total squared Euclidean distance from every point to its own centroid. Smaller means tighter clusters.

### 5.4 Standardise first, or distance lies

Inertia is a sum of **squared feature differences**, so a feature on a large scale dominates it whether or not it matters. Income runs in tens of thousands while the debt ratio sits between 0 and 1: unscaled, the clustering becomes **a sort on income alone**. Standardise every feature (subtract the mean, divide by the standard deviation) so each gets a comparable vote. It is not cosmetic: it **silently decides which features influence the result**. When a clustering looks like a sort on one column, suspect the scaling before you believe the finding.

### 5.5 The assumption is the model

Every call to K-means asserts three things. **Round, equal clusters**: it expects spherical groups of similar spread, and long, thin or unequal ones confuse it. **Hard membership**: every point belongs fully to exactly one cluster, with no borderline cases. **Scale sensitivity**: “near” means Euclidean distance, so scaling is part of the model. When the claim is wrong, K-means does not warn you; it returns a **clean, confident partition that misreads the shape**. And every clustering method returns an answer on any data at all: hand K-means uniform noise, ask for three clusters, and you get three, tidy and confident.

### 5.6 Choosing K: the elbow, the silhouette, and your defence

There is **no true K** waiting in the data; K is an argument you make. The **elbow**: inertia always falls as K grows, so seek the bend, not the minimum. On the credit data it runs 13,530 at K = 2, 10,630 at K = 3, 8,638 at K = 4 and about 6,000 at K = 8: a soft bend near three, no clean corner, which is normal for real data. The **silhouette**, s = (b − a) ÷ max(a, b), compares a point’s distance to its own cluster (a) with the nearest other (b), from −1 to +1: near +1 well placed, near 0 on the border, below 0 probably misassigned. It peaks at K = 2 (0.348), then 0.311 and 0.265. The tools **disagree** because the elbow rewards tightness and the silhouette rewards separation. And 0.35 is **weak**: real but soft structure. Print both, choose, and **defend the choice in a sentence**; the per-point knife plot shows the fence-sitters the mean hides.

### 5.7 Hierarchical clustering and Gaussian mixtures

**Hierarchical** (agglomerative) clustering starts with every point alone and repeatedly merges the two closest clusters, recording a tree, the **dendrogram**. The height of each join is how reluctant the merge was. You **cut the tree** to get clusters, so K is still your decision, made visible. Two knobs reshape it: the **distance** between points and the **linkage** between clusters (Ward merges the pair that adds least inertia). A **Gaussian mixture model** (GMM) blends K Gaussian bells, each with its own centre, tilted elliptical shape and weight, and gives **soft membership**: an applicant can be 78% one cluster and 22% another. K-means is the special case with equal spheres and 0-or-1 membership. The near-50/50 cases are exactly the ones a human should review, and a GMM’s likelihood lets you compare K with the **BIC**.

### 5.8 What cluster validity can and cannot tell you

**Internal** measures (silhouette, inertia) judge the geometry alone: tight and separated? They need no ground truth, and they are **blind to whether the clusters mean anything**. **External** measures compare clusters with known labels; they speak to meaning, but need labels you usually do not have. The trap: a high silhouette certifies a **tidy** partition, not a useful or fair one, and not one that is free of scaling artefacts. So the last word in unsupervised learning is never a metric; it is **a human reading the clusters against the domain**.

### 5.9 High dimensions, and PCA

The **curse of dimensionality**: as features multiply, points spread until nearly everything is equally far from everything else, “nearest” loses its force, and every distance-based method weakens. **PCA** (principal component analysis) discards the spreadsheet’s axes and finds the direction of **greatest variance** (PC1), then the next at right angles (PC2), and so on. Mathematically the components are the **eigenvectors of the covariance matrix**, and each eigenvalue is the variance along its component. The **explained variance ratio** is each component’s share: on the credit data PC1 holds 66.2% and PC2 19.7%, 85.9% together, so dropping to two axes costs about 14%. The assumption you accept: **variance is where the signal lives**. Often true, not always: a rare, low-variance direction may be exactly the fraud you were hired to find, and PCA drops it first.

### 5.10 Name the axes: loadings

Each component is a weighted blend of the original features, and the weights are its **loadings**; a **biplot** draws them as arrows. On the credit data PC1’s loadings are income +0.549, credit history +0.547, debt ratio −0.533 and prior defaults −0.339: high income and long history at one end, high debt and past defaults at the other. That is not “PC1”; it is a **financial-health axis** the data drew for itself. Translating loadings into a sentence a domain expert would accept is the skill being assessed. **Leave the axes unnamed and you have decorated, not interpreted.**

### 5.11 PCA is your first learned representation, and its straight-axis limit

PCA returned a **new coordinate system fitted to the data**. The applicant did not change; their **description** did, from four measured quantities to two coordinates on axes no human chose. That learned description is a **representation**, and everything downstream (clustering, plots, the next model) now runs on it, so it quietly shapes all of them. Ask by reflex: “what representation is this running on, and what did its builder bake in?” But PCA is **linear**. Two interleaved arcs are obviously separable by eye, yet projected onto PC1 they land on top of each other: **the variance was real, it just was not the signal**. When PCA fails, it fails quietly, which is why nonlinear methods and learned embeddings exist.

### 5.12 Embeddings: geometry as meaning

Drop PCA’s demands that the axes be straight, few and nameable, and what remains is an **embedding**: a learned map placing each item (an applicant, a word, an image) as a point where **position carries meaning**. **Distance** becomes similarity: nearest-neighbour search, recommendation and retrieval all run on it. **Direction** becomes relationship: in a good word embedding, king − man + woman lands near queen, learned rather than programmed. **The representation is the product**: modern systems spend most effort learning it, and its quality caps everything above. Trade-offs: PCA is fast and interpretable but misses curves; **t-SNE and UMAP** show neighbourhoods beautifully, but **the gaps between clusters can be meaningless**; learned embeddings are expressive but opaque and hard to audit.

### 5.13 Representations encode bias

Colour the same PCA map by the group column deleted before any algorithm ran, and the groups **separate**, mostly along PC1. Measured: PC1 alone recovers the group **71.1%** of the time against a **42.6%** base rate, almost exactly the 70% the supervised score leaked in Weeks 3 and 4. **Removing the column removed the word, not the information.** (The hard two-way K-means split matches the group only weakly, with an adjusted Rand index of 0.156: the clusters are not the group; the continuous axis carries it.) Why: an unequal world, so correlated features, so the largest axis of variance runs along the inequality, and PCA hunts exactly that. No malice, no bug. A representation is **never neutral**, and the silhouette, which sees shape not justice, could never have flagged it. “We removed that field” describes your schema, not your geometry.

## The nine questions before you believe a cluster or an axis

You may be asked to apply these, so read each one as a test you could run.

| # | Question |
|---|---|
| 1 | Did I standardise first, and would a different scaling change the answer? |
| 2 | Which K did I choose, and can I defend it when the elbow and the silhouette disagree? |
| 3 | What shape does my method assume: round balls, a cut tree, or tilted ellipses? |
| 4 | Would this method have handed me the same tidy answer on pure noise? |
| 5 | What does each cluster mean in the domain, and which ones are shaky? |
| 6 | What is each axis made of? Can I name PC1 from its loadings? |
| 7 | Is the structure I care about linear, or would a straight axis flatten it? |
| 8 | Can a simple probe recover a sensitive attribute from my representation, against what base rate? |
| 9 | Which structure would I refuse to act on, and why? |
